Corporate lending isn't what it used to be. For decades, big banks like JPMorgan Chase and Goldman Sachs were the gatekeepers, intermediating capital, funding loans, and underwriting corporate risk. But that dominance has been quietly eroding.

Private credit has stepped up to fill the gap, with firms like Blackstone, Apollo, and KKR now lending directly to mid-sized and large companies, often faster and more flexibly than banks can. According to Reuters, just last month, private credit in the United States sat at a staggering $2 trillion! But this shift hasn't come out of nowhere. Banks, weighed down by tighter rules and capital requirements, have been retreating from riskier corporate loans for years, opening the door for non-bank lenders to step in and fill unmet demand.

But this growth isn't without its critics. In late February, Reuters also highlighted strains in the private credit market, including stress at major lenders like Blue Owl, which underscored concerns about liquidity and risk transparency as the sector expands.

For borrowers, private credit represents choice and capital that banks may no longer provide. For markets, it signals a deeper shift: capital allocation in corporate lending is no longer controlled only by banks.
