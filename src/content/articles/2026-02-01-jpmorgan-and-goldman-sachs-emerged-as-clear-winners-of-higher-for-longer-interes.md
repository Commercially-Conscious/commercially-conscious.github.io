As inflation in the United States began to gradually ease through 2025, many investors expected the Federal Reserve to quickly move towards interest rate cuts. By December, the Fed had delivered a series of modest cuts, bringing its benchmark interest rate to the 3,50 to 3,75% range.

However, Fed officials, including Chair Jerome Powell, signaled that future reductions would be limited and that rates may remain higher for longer - a strategy adopted by central banks, especially the Federal Reserve, as a measure to prevent renewed inflationary pressures after premature rate cuts. While this has increased pressure on borrowers and slowed certain sectors of the economy, some of the world's largest banks, particularly JPMorgan and Goldman Sachs, have quietly benefitted.

One of the main reasons banks benefit from higher interest rates is through an expansion in net interest margins (NIMs). More simply, banks earn more when the difference between the interest charged on loans and the interest paid on deposits widens. As rates rise, loan yields increase faster than deposit costs, allowing banks to earn more per dollar lent. According to Reuters, JPMorgan and Goldman Sachs have been such banks to effectively capitalize on this and have continued to experience strong net interest income even as overall economic growth moderates.

Although it has favoured large banks, mortgage rates, credit card interest, and borrowing costs have all risen sharply, reducing demand for new loans and increasing financial strain on households and businesses. This divergence highlights a key feature of monetary tightening cycles: while aiming to slow inflation, clear winners and losers are often created. As long as rates remain elevated, earnings for institutions like JPMorgan and Goldman Sachs are likely to remain supported.
